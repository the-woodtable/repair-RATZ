Traversal Subsystem — Obstacle Traversal Analysis

EDI Pipe Cam · Group 12 · Subsystem analysis (wheels)

1. Objective

Determine whether the available thrust is sufficient to overcome obstacles in the pipe, and identify the maximum obstacle steepness the robot can traverse before the wheels slip.

The analysis predicts the limiting ramp angle \(\theta_{\max}\) the six-wheel canted drive can climb, establishes which physical mechanism sets that limit (traction versus motor torque), and validates the prediction against a measured drawbar-pull test.

Measurable requirement addressed: the robot shall traverse a 3 m pipe section containing surface obstacles (deployed liner edge, rust and debris) without stalling.

3. Assumptions

  1. Contact and friction. Each wheel rolls purely along its canted direction and is restrained laterally by Coulomb friction, with a single coefficient \(\mu\) identical at all six contacts and static and kinetic values taken as equal. Lateral slip occurs only once \(\mu N\) is exceeded — locating that limit is the purpose of this analysis.
  2. Suspension and load sharing. The carrier is rigid in rotation, while each wheel is independently sprung on a radial carriage of rate \(k_w = 0.119\) N/mm bearing against a rigid, ideally circular pipe wall. All six drive wheels remain in contact and share load per the ring model of §5 — an assumption verified against measurement in §7. The front module's passive wheels react the drive reaction torque and contribute no axial thrust.
  3. Load cases. Rolling resistance and obstacle climbing are the only resistive forces considered. Obstacles are modelled as spanning the full circumference, so all six wheels engage simultaneously — the worst case. Tether drag and hydrodynamic effects are neglected, and conditions are dry throughout.
  4. Quasi-static operation with ideal transmission. The robot advances at a constant 31 mm/s, so inertial and acceleration effects are negligible. The gear train is treated as ideal; real drivetrain losses are absorbed into the measured stall force and therefore do not inflate the traction result.

4. Reference frames and kinematics

Frames: \(z\) along the pipe axis, \(r\) radially outward, \(\phi\) circumferential measured from the upward vertical.

Because the wheels are canted, the drive behaves as a screw. In one revolution of the wheel carrier each contact traverses the full circumference \(2\pi R_p\) while the robot advances \(p\) axially:

$$\tan\beta = \frac{p}{2\pi R_p} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p = 2\pi R_p\tan\beta$$

The cant angle is fixed by the build at \(\beta = 18.3°\), so with the measured 98 mm pipe radius:

$$p = 2\pi(98)\tan 18.3° = 615.8 \times 0.3305 = 203.5\ \text{mm/rev}$$

Note: the mechanism was originally canted for 200 mm/rev on an assumed 96 mm radius. The test pipe measures 98 mm, so the geometric advance is 1.8 % larger than the design intent. §10 shows this has no effect on obstacle capability.

Drive chain

The stepper delivers 200 full steps per revolution through a \(G = 3\) reduction, so one carrier revolution is \(200 \times 3 = 600\) steps. At the operating rate of 100 steps/s the carrier turns at 10 rpm.

Measured advance and slip

In-pipe calibration over 7 runs gave 12925 steps per 4012 mm, i.e. 3.222 steps/mm. Converting to advance per carrier revolution:

$$p_{\text{meas}} = \frac{600}{3.222} = 186.2\ \text{mm/rev}$$

Comparing against the geometric value gives the wheel slip directly:

$$s = 1 - \frac{186.2}{203.5} = \mathbf{8.5\%}$$

5. Contact statics — load sharing around the ring

The six wheels sit at \(\phi_i = 0°, 60°, \ldots, 300°\) on radial spring carriages. Unlike a ground vehicle, the normal force comes from spring preload, not from weight; gravity only redistributes it around the ring.

Let the drive module sag by \(\delta\). Wheel \(i\)'s spring compression changes by \(-\delta\cos\phi_i\), so \(N_i = N_{\text{pre}} - k_w\delta\cos\phi_i\). Vertical equilibrium, using \(\sum\cos\phi_i = 0\) and \(\sum\cos^2\phi_i = n/2\), gives \(k_w\delta = 2m_dg/n\) and therefore

$$\boxed{\;N_i = N_{\text{pre}} - \frac{2m_dg}{n}\cos\phi_i\;}$$

(a) Contact retention sets a minimum preload. The top wheel carries least, so all six stay in contact only if

$$N_{\text{pre}} > \frac{2m_dg}{n} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m_d < \frac{3N_{\text{pre}}}{g} = \frac{3(1.96)}{9.81} = 0.60\ \text{kg}$$

(b) The total normal force is independent of weight. Since \(\sum\cos\phi_i = 0\),

$$\sum N_i = nN_{\text{pre}} = 6(1.96) = 11.76\ \text{N}$$

Spring characterisation

Springs are 0.3 × 5 × 30 mm music wire, 13 active coils, two per carriage. Mean coil diameter \(D = 4.7\) mm:

$$k_s = \frac{Gd^4}{8D^3n_a} = \frac{79300 \times 0.3^4}{8 \times 4.7^3 \times 13} = 0.0595\ \text{N/mm}, \qquad k_w = 2k_s = 0.119\ \text{N/mm}$$

The measured 1.96 N preload corresponds to 16.5 mm of compression, within the ~25.5 mm available before the coils go solid. The independent calculation and the direct measurement agree, validating both.

6. Drivetrain thrust

Motor torque is stepped up through the gear train and delivered to the carrier:

$$\tau_{\text{carrier}} = G\,\tau_m = 3 \times 0.44 = 1.32\ \text{N·m}\;(132\ \text{N·cm})$$

This torque is reacted by the contact forces at the pipe wall, at radius \(R_p\):

$$\sum F_c = \frac{\tau_{\text{carrier}}}{R_p} = \frac{1.32}{0.098} = 13.5\ \mathrm{N} \quad\text{(total, six wheels)}$$

\(F_c\) and \(F_a\) are components of the same contact reaction \(F_R\), with \(F_c = F_R\sin\beta\) and \(F_a = F_R\cos\beta\), so \(F_a = F_c/\tan\beta\):

$$F_{\text{thrust}} = \frac{\sum F_c}{\tan\beta} = \frac{13.5}{0.3305} = 40.8\ \mathrm{N}$$

Combined into a single expression:

$$\boxed{\;F_{\text{thrust}} = \frac{G\,\tau_m}{R_p\tan\beta} = 40.8\ \mathrm{N}\;}$$

Energy cross-check, independent of any moment arm or force resolution: \(F = 2\pi\tau_{\text{carrier}}/p = 2\pi(1.32)/0.2035 = 40.8\) N. The gear-mesh tooth force \(\tau_{\text{carrier}}/r_g = 18.6\) N is a separate quantity and does not enter this chain, since the thrust reaction occurs at the pipe wall rather than at the gear teeth.

7. Traction limit and the governing criterion

Each canted wheel rolls freely along its canted axis and is restrained laterally by friction. The reaction \(F_R\) that produces thrust acts perpendicular to the rolling direction and is limited by the available friction:

$$F_R \le \mu N_i, \qquad F_{a,i} = F_R\cos\beta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_{a,i} \le \mu N_i\cos\beta$$ $$F_{a,\max} = \mu\cos\beta\sum N_i$$

To climb a ramp of angle \(\theta\), the axial force required is \(N_i\tan\theta\) per wheel, so total demand is \(\tan\theta\sum N_i\). Setting demand equal to capability:

Governing criterion — \(\sum N_i\) cancels:

$$\tan\theta\sum N_i \le \mu\cos\beta\sum N_i \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;\theta_{\max} = \arctan(\mu\cos\beta)\;}$$

This is a non-dimensional result. Maximum obstacle steepness depends only on friction coefficient and cant angle — independent of robot mass, spring preload, wheel radius and pipe diameter, which makes it robust to exactly the variations hardest to control.

With the measured \(\mu = 0.88\) and \(\beta = 18.3°\):

$$\theta_{\max} = \arctan(0.88 \times 0.9495) = \arctan(0.834) = \mathbf{39.8°}$$

Special case — the liner edge (vertical step)

The deployed liner presents a square step rather than a ramp. This is the \(\theta \to 90°\) limit, in which the wheel contacts a corner and wheel radius re-enters:

$$\frac{F}{N} = \frac{\sqrt{2r_wh - h^2}}{r_w - h} \;\le\; \mu\cos\beta$$

At \(h = 3.5\) mm with \(r_w = 23.9\) mm the requirement is \(0.610\) against \(0.834\) available — a safety factor of 1.37. Solving for the limit gives \(h_{\max} = 5.5\) mm.

Design recommendation. A 1.37 margin on the square-step case is the tightest number in this analysis. Chamfering the liner's leading edge converts the step into a ramp: 3.5 mm feathered over 10 mm is a 19.3° slope, comfortably inside the 39.8° capability and raising the margin above 2. This is a liner-geometry change, not a drivetrain change, and it costs nothing.

8. Experimental validation — drawbar pull test

Method

  1. Robot placed in the horizontal test pipe and commanded to drive forward at 100 steps/s.
  2. A line attached to the rear of the robot was restrained axially through a force scale.
  3. Restraining load increased progressively until axial advance ceased.
  4. The carrier was observed to continue rotating while the robot remained stationary, confirming a traction limit and not a motor stall.
  5. Peak restraining force recorded as \(F_{\text{stall}}\).

Result and inversion

$$F_{\text{stall}} = 9.81\ \text{N} \qquad \mu = \frac{F_{\text{stall}}}{\sum N_i\cos\beta} = \frac{9.81}{6(1.96)(0.9495)} = \mathbf{0.88}$$

This single measurement supplies the only parameter in the governing criterion that cannot be derived from geometry or component specification, and it simultaneously confirms that the binding constraint is traction rather than torque.

Validation of the contact assumption

Assumption 2 (all six wheels in contact) can be checked against the measurement. The stall test implies \(\sum N_i = F_{\text{stall}}/(\mu\cos\beta) = 11.76\) N, matching \(6N_{\text{pre}} = 11.76\) N. Had only three wheels been loaded, the implied \(\mu\) would exceed 1.7, which is not physically plausible for TPU on PVC. The assumption holds, and by §5(a) the drive module mass share must be at or below 0.60 kg.

The design is traction-limited with 4.2× torque margin remaining, so performance is bounded by wheel grip rather than by the drive — a more powerful motor would not climb steeper.

10. Sensitivity and limitations

Robustness to pipe diameter

The test pipe measured 196 mm rather than the 192 mm assumed at design time — a 2 mm change in radius. Propagating this through:

This is a real demonstration rather than a hypothetical one: the pipe turned out 4 mm wider than designed for, and the obstacle capability did not move at all. The specification's ±5 mm diameter tolerance is therefore not a risk to traversal performance.

The one consequence of a diameter change is a proportional error in odometry-reported position: over a 3 m run, ±2.5 mm on radius produces ±76 mm of position error if the calibration constant is not updated. This is mitigated by re-calibrating STEPS_PER_MM per pipe, or by correcting the stepper odometry against the stereo camera's absolute range fix.

Limitations

  • \(\mu = 0.88\) rests on a single stall measurement and sits at the upper end of the plausible range for TPU on PVC. Repeating the test 3–5 times would provide an error bar; results should be treated as a nominal case pending that.
  • Wet traction is out of scope. Since the test rig is water-filled in other trials, wet performance should be characterised separately before any in-service claim.
  • Tether drag is neglected. Over longer runs it adds to the axial demand and would reduce the effective \(\theta_{\max}\).
  • The model treats a single obstacle. Sustained rough surfaces, where several wheels engage different features simultaneously, are not covered.
  • Rolling resistance uses a literature \(C_{rr}\), but contributes under 4 % of the traction limit, so the result is insensitive to it.

Recommended follow-up test. Build ramps at 35° and 45° and run the robot at each. The model predicts the first is cleared and the second is not. Confirming a prediction made in advance is substantially stronger evidence than a measurement taken alone.
